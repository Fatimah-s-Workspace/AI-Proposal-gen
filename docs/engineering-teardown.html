Part 0

What the thing actually is

The problem, told as a story

Imagine a huge competition. A hospital needs someone to supply 10,000 beds, and whoever wins gets paid a lot of money. To enter, you must send in an application.

The hospital publishes a rulebook. It is 80 pages long. Buried inside are rules like:

  • "You must attach your tax certificate."
  • "Applications sent after 3pm on Friday will not be accepted."
  • "You must show three similar jobs you have done before."

Here is the cruel part: if you miss even one rule, they throw your application in the bin without reading it. All that work, gone, because of one sentence on page 61 that nobody noticed.

In real life this rulebook is called a tender pack, and this happens to real companies constantly.

What our program does about it

TenderFlow AI is a helper that:

  1. Reads the rulebook for you. You give it the file; it reads all 80 pages.
  2. Finds every rule. It hunts for the sentences that are actually orders,

and ignores the waffle.

  1. Turns each rule into a checklist item. Like a packing list before a trip.
  2. Notices what you have already got. If you upload your tax certificate,

it ticks off the rule that asked for a tax certificate.

  1. Helps you write the application, pointing at each rule as it goes.
  2. Gives you a score — "you are 72% ready" — so you know if you are safe to

send it.

  1. Makes the final document you actually submit.

The single most important idea in the whole program

Look at that fork in the middle. That is the most important decision in this entire program, so let us be clear about why it is there.

AI is not always available. Sometimes the AI service is broken. Sometimes it is too busy. Sometimes we have used up our free allowance for the month. Sometimes the internet is simply down.

Most programs would just show an error and give up. Ours does not. If the AI cannot help, the program uses its own set of ordinary recipes — no AI, no internet — and still gives you a full, usable answer. The AI makes it better; it is not what makes it work.

The rule to remember: anything that depends on someone else can fail. So always have a plan B — and make plan B good enough to live with.

You will see this same idea four more times in this guide: for storing files, for counting requests, for checking passwords, and for sending email. It is the single most useful habit in the whole codebase.

What the program is made of

  • One program doing everything. Not lots of little separate programs talking

to each other. Fewer moving parts, fewer things to break.

  • A database to remember things. A database is just a very organised set of

labelled boxes that never forgets what you put in them.

  • Most of the work happens on the server. More on what that means in Part 3.
  • No waiting queue. Slow jobs run after the page has already replied to

you, so you are never left staring at a spinner.

Part 1

The app, screen by screen

This is a walk through every page, in the order a real person meets them. For each one: what you see, what you do, and what is secretly happening underneath.

Terms you need for this part:

A page is one screen in the app. Its URL (or route) is the address you type — like /dashboard. Public pages can be seen by anyone. Private pages check who you are first and send strangers away.

1.1 / — The front door (public)

The first thing a stranger sees. Its only job is to answer "what is this and should I care?" in about ten seconds.

It has four blocks, in this order, and the order is deliberate:

  1. The promise. "Turn tender packs into compliant proposal drafts without

losing the trail of evidence." One sentence saying what you get.

  1. Six features — Tender intelligence, Compliance checklist, Plain-English

clauses, AI proposal drafting, Evidence matching, Export-ready output.

  1. How it works, in four steps: Create a project → Upload the tender pack →

Review the checklist → Draft and export. This is the whole product in four words-and-a-bit, so nobody has to guess.

  1. Who it is for — SMEs & contractors, NGOs & impact teams, BD &

procurement teams. People decide "is this for someone like me?" faster than they decide "is this good?"

Underneath: this page is pre-rendered, meaning the server builds the finished page and sends it. Your browser does almost no work, so it appears fast even on a slow phone on a slow network — which matters, because most of the people this is built for are on exactly that.

1.2 /auth/sign-up — Making an account (public)

A form: full name, country (Nigeria by default), organisation, email, password.

Watch the password box as you type. Underneath it, five little rules light up green one by one:

  • At least 8 characters
  • A lowercase letter
  • An uppercase letter
  • A number
  • A symbol

Why show them live? Because the alternative is cruel. You type a password, press the button, and then get told it was wrong — and now you have to work out which of five rules you broke. Showing the rules as you type means you never fail at all.

There is also a small eye icon in the password box. Click it and your password becomes readable. This exists because a password you cannot see is a password you can typo, and a typo in a new password locks you out of an account you just made.

Underneath, when you press "Create account":

  1. The rules are checked again on the server. (Never trust the browser — see

Part 3.)

  1. Your password is checked against a list of **passwords stolen in real data

breaches**. If yours is on it, you are asked for a different one.

  1. Your account is made, along with your own private workspace.
  2. A confirmation email is sent.

You are not signed in automatically. You have to confirm your email first. Part 6 explains why we chose that, and what it costs.

1.3 /auth/sign-in — Coming back (public)

Email, password, "Sign in". A link to reset a forgotten password.

Three things are happening that you cannot see:

  • Wrong password? You get: *"That email and password combination doesn't

match an account."* Notice it does not say whether the email exists. That is on purpose, and Part 3 explains the attack it prevents.

  • Too many tries? After 8 wrong attempts on the same account, it stops

accepting them for a few minutes.

  • Email not confirmed yet? You get told so — and a button appears offering

to send you a fresh confirmation link.

1.4 /auth/forgot-password and /auth/reset-password

You type your email. The page says: "If that address has an account, a reset link is on its way."

Read that sentence again. It says "if". It says the same thing whether or not you actually have an account. A stranger typing in your email learns nothing at all.

The reset link works once and expires after an hour. When you use it, you pick a new password — and it goes through exactly the same rules and the same breached-password check as sign-up, because a reset is the moment people are most tempted to reuse an old password from somewhere else.

1.5 /auth/verify-email — Confirming it is you

You land here from the link in your email. It checks the link and stamps your account as confirmed.

The link is single-use and expires after 24 hours. If it has expired, you are told plainly and offered a new one, rather than being left at a dead end.

1.6 /invite/[token] — Joining someone else's team

Somebody invited you. This page reads the invite code from the address bar, shows you which team you have been invited to, and lets you accept.

If you do not have an account yet, it sends you to sign-up with your email already filled in, so you cannot accidentally join with the wrong address.

1.7 /dashboard — Home base (private)

The first thing you see after signing in. It answers "what is going on?" at a glance:

  • Four counters across the top: Active projects, Uploaded documents,

Requirements tracked, Proposal sections.

Underneath: the counters come from the database in one go when the page is built, not from lots of little requests after it loads. That is why it appears complete instead of filling in piece by piece.

1.8 /projects — All your work (private)

Every project, with a compliance percentage next to each. There is a search box so you can find one by name once you have more than a handful.

What is a project? One bid for one contract. It holds the rulebook you uploaded, the checklist that came out of it, the proposal you are writing, and every version you exported.

1.9 /projects/new — Starting a bid (private)

A form describing what you are bidding for: title, client, opportunity type (Tender, RFP, Grant, EOI, RFQ), sector, country, deadline, budget range.

Then some optional but powerful fields: evaluation criteria, win themes, contract duration, and whether there is an incumbent (the company doing the job now).

Those last ones are not decoration. They get fed to the AI when it drafts your proposal, so it can write toward the things the buyer actually scores you on. A generic proposal loses to a targeted one.

1.10 /projects/[id] — One project's home (private)

The hub for a single bid. Shows the project's details, its documents, its compliance score, and links to everywhere else.

This is also where you upload the tender pack. Drag a PDF, DOCX or TXT in. The moment it lands, the whole pipeline from Part 0 starts running.

You get a reply immediately — the file is saved and accepted — while the slow reading and analysing carries on in the background. You are not made to wait.

1.11 /projects/[id]/parsed-overview — What the computer found

The most interesting screen in the app. After the rulebook is analysed, this shows you the results:

  • Parsed documents — what was read, and whether it worked.
  • Checklist items — every rule found, what type it is, and how risky it is.
  • Evidence matches — which of your uploaded documents seem to satisfy which

rules.

Each clause comes with a plain-English explanation. Instead of "The Bidder shall furnish a Bid Security in the sum of 2% of the Bid Price...", you get told what that means and what to do about it.

If a document could not be read, this page says so, in words, with a reason — usually "this is a scanned picture of text, not text". That is far kinder than silently showing you an empty checklist.

1.12 /projects/[id]/workspace — Writing the proposal (private)

The word processor. The left side lists your proposal's sections; the middle is where you write; the AI tools sit alongside.

You can ask it to draft against the checklist — it reads your requirements and writes sections aimed at them, then tells you which rules each section covers and which are still missing.

Your edits save as you work, and old versions are kept, so you can go back.

1.13 /projects/[id]/export — Making the final document

Choose DOCX (Word) or PDF. The file is built and downloaded.

Every export starts with a cover sheet: project title, client, reference number, date. An evaluator opening your file should know who is bidding, for what, before reading a single word of prose.

Past exports are listed, so you can always get back the exact file you sent.

1.14 /projects/[id]/edit — Changing project details

Same form as creating, filled in with what is already there. Boring, and it should be — the interesting thing is that it reuses the identical form component, so the two screens can never drift apart.

Every meaningful action anyone in your team took, in plain sentences: "Updated a checklist requirement", "Ran the draft assistant", "Joined the team", "Upgraded to the Pro plan". Filterable.

Part 2

Data structures and algorithms

This is the thinking part. Two words first:

A data structure is a way of arranging information so it is easy to work with. A shopping list is a data structure. So is a family tree. An algorithm is a recipe: a fixed set of steps that turns some input into some output. Long division is an algorithm. So is "how to tie a shoelace".

Everything in this part lives in two files, and both are pure — they take information in and give an answer back, without touching the internet or the database. That makes them the easiest part of the program to test, which is not an accident: it is why they were written that way.

2.1 Chopping a document into sections

The problem. A PDF gives us one enormous blob of text. We need it split into sections — "Eligibility", "Submission", "Evaluation" — because a rule means different things depending on which section it is in.

The idea. Walk through the text one line at a time. Decide for each line: is this a heading, or is this ordinary text? If it is a heading, start a new section. If not, add it to whatever section we are currently in.

How we spot a heading. Two clues:

  1. It is short — under 90 characters. Headings are short; paragraphs are not.
  2. It either looks like a title (starts with a capital, maybe numbered like

"3.1") or starts with one of nine words we know show up as headings in these documents: scope, eligibility, submission, evaluation, financial, technical, terms, instructions, requirements.

for each line:
    if line is short AND looks like a title:
        finish the current section
        start a new section with this line as its name
    otherwise:
        add this line to the current section

The data structure is just two things: a box we are currently filling (current), and a shelf of finished boxes (sections). One pass through the text. If the document doubles in length, the work doubles — no worse. That is what people mean by O(n): "grows in a straight line with the input".

The bug this design avoids. What if the document starts with a paragraph before any heading? With a naive version, those lines have no box to go in and get silently thrown away. So the code creates a box called "Tender overview" to catch them. Losing the first page of a document, silently, is exactly the kind of bug that survives for years because nothing ever complains.

Why not something cleverer? Real PDF layout tools can look at font sizes and positions to find headings properly. They are also large, slow, and often fail on the badly-made PDFs that government offices produce. Our two clues are wrong sometimes — and being wrong just means a section boundary in an odd place, which a human can see and ignore. We picked the failure we could live with.

2.2 Chopping sections into individual rules

A section is still a wall of text. We need it broken into single rules, because each rule becomes one checklist item.

Stage one: split into sentences. Not on every full stop — that breaks "Ltd." and "3.1" into pieces. Instead: split at a space only when there is a full stop before it and a capital letter or digit after it.

Stage two: split the monsters. Legal writing chains several obligations into one endless sentence:

"The bidder shall submit a tax certificate; provide audited accounts for three years; and attach evidence of similar work."

That is three separate things to do. If any sentence is longer than 420 characters, we split it again — at semicolons, and at commas followed by and, or, or provided.

That number, 420, and that list of words, are domain knowledge: things we know about how procurement documents are written, written down as instructions. This is a real and underrated skill. Knowing your subject is worth more than knowing a clever technique.

Finally, each piece is capped at 900 characters. Always put a limit on things.

2.3 Deciding what kind of rule it is

We have a rule. What sort is it? We check it against a list of questions, in order, and the first one that matches wins:

Does it mention deadlines, submitting, portals, sealed envelopes?  → submission
Does it mention money, prices, bid security, bank guarantees?      → financial
Does it mention methodology, work plans, scope, personnel?         → technical
Does it mention CAC, tax, PENCOM, registration, eligibility?       → eligibility
Does it mention experience, similar assignments, past performance? → experience
Does it mention conflicts, anti-corruption, fraud, contracts?      → legal
Does it mention evaluation, criteria, scoring?                     → evaluation
None of the above?                                                 → instruction

This is called a decision list, and it is the simplest classifier there is. Four things about it are worth learning:

The order is the actual intelligence. Whatever is checked first wins. We check "submission" before "technical" because a deadline hidden inside a technical section is still a deadline, and missing a deadline is fatal. Reordering these lines changes the program's behaviour more than rewriting any of them.

There is always an answer. The last line catches everything else. No rule can ever come out unlabelled and vanish.

You can always explain it. Ask "why did it call this financial?" and you can point at the exact line. Try that with a big AI model — you cannot. For something a person has to trust with their livelihood, explainability is a feature.

It deliberately over-worries. Risk is marked "high" if the text contains must, shall, required, mandatory, disqualify — or if it is one of four high-stakes types. It flags too much. That is the right way round: a false "high risk" costs someone five minutes of reading. A false "low risk" can lose them the contract.

2.4 How sure are we? — confidence scoring

Every rule gets a confidence number. Start at 0.58, then add:

If the rule...Add
contains must, shall, required, submit, provide, attach, include+0.22
mentions deadlines, eligibility, technical, financial, tax, CAC, bid security+0.12
is longer than 80 characters+0.06

Then cap it at 0.97.

Two deliberate choices. It never reaches 1.0 — a guessing recipe should never claim to be certain. It never starts at 0 — anything that got this far already matched our "this is an instruction" test, so it has earned a base score.

Later, when a rule becomes a checklist item, its confidence drops by 0.03. Every extra step of reasoning is another chance to be wrong, and the number should admit that.

2.5 Removing duplicates — the fingerprint trick

The same requirement often appears three times in one rulebook, worded slightly differently. Showing it three times makes the checklist useless.

The idea: give every rule a fingerprint. If two fingerprints match, it is the same rule.

Making a fingerprint:

"Bid Security: 2% of the Bid Price!"
        │  make it all lowercase
        ▼
"bid security: 2% of the bid price!"
        │  turn every punctuation mark and space run into one space
        ▼
"bid security 2 of the bid price "
        │  trim, then keep only the first 120 characters
        ▼
"bid security 2 of the bid price"          ← the fingerprint

Now "Bid Security — 2 % of the Bid Price" produces the same fingerprint. Different typing, same rule, caught.

Then we walk the list holding a bag of fingerprints we have already seen:

seen = empty bag
for each rule:
    f = fingerprint(rule)
    if f is already in the bag:  throw this rule away
    otherwise:                   keep it, and put f in the bag

The bag is a hash set — a container that answers "have I seen this before?" instantly, no matter how many things are in it. That instant answer is why the whole job takes one pass instead of comparing every rule against every other rule. For 100 rules that is 100 checks instead of 4,950.

Why only the first 120 characters? A trade-off, chosen on purpose. Using the whole sentence would miss near-duplicates where an addendum restates a rule with a different ending. Using a short prefix catches those — but occasionally merges two genuinely different rules that begin the same way. In this domain the same rule really does get repeated, so we err toward merging.

The best detail here: this fingerprint function is exported and reused. It runs both within a single upload and against rules already stored from earlier uploads. If those two used different fingerprinting, uploading the same file twice would create duplicates that the first upload had carefully removed. One shared function, one definition of "the same".

2.6 Matching your documents to the rules

You have uploaded a tax certificate. Which rules does it satisfy?

The naive approach — compare the document's text to the rule's text — works badly, because they are written in totally different language.

Our approach: give both sides a common vocabulary. We keep a small dictionary of about ten kinds of evidence, each with the words that hint at it:

Evidence typeHint words
Tax clearancetax clearance, TIN, FIRS, VAT
CAC registrationCAC, corporate affairs, registration
Bid securitybid security, bank guarantee, tender security, bond
Key personnel CVsCV, curriculum vitae, key personnel, team lead
...and six more

Then, for every rule and every evidence type, we ask two questions:

  1. Does the rule ask for this kind of evidence?
  2. Does the document look like this kind of evidence?

Only if both are yes is it a match. Neither side is ever compared to the other directly — they are both translated into the same ten-word vocabulary first, and matched there. That is what makes it survive completely different wording.

Confidence is simple: if more than one hint word appears in the document, 0.86; otherwise 0.72. A document mentioning both "tax clearance" and "FIRS" is more likely to really be one than a document that says "VAT" once.

We keep only the single best match per rule — a greedy choice, meaning we take the best-looking option without reconsidering later.

One lovely detail: when you re-upload a document, its old match is removed before the new one is added. So processing the same file five times leaves you with one match, not five. Doing something twice giving the same result as doing it once is called idempotence, and it prevents an enormous class of bugs.

2.7 The readiness score

This is the percentage you see on your dashboard, so it deserves care. It combines two separate ideas:

How much does this rule matter?

PriorityWeight
High1.35
Medium1.00
Low0.75

How done is it?

StatusCredit
Met1.00
Waiver (officially excused)0.85
In progress0.45
Not started0.25
Blocked0.00

Then:

score = (sum of weight × credit)  ÷  (sum of weight)  × 100

A worked example. Three rules:

RulePriorityStatusWeightCreditWeight × Credit
Tax certificateHighMet1.351.001.35
Three referencesMediumIn progress1.000.450.45
Company brochureLowNot started0.750.250.19
3.101.99

1.99 ÷ 3.10 = 0.642 → 64%.

Three decisions inside that formula are worth understanding:

We divide by total weight, not by how many rules there are. That is what makes 64% mean the same thing on a project with 8 rules and one with 80.

Blocked scores zero, never negative. A blocked rule pulls the score down by removing its own weight from the top of the fraction — but it cannot drag you below what your other work earned. If it could, doing more work could make your score go down, and users rightly find that maddening.

A waiver is 0.85, not 1.00. Officially excused is not the same as done. There is a little risk left, and the number quietly says so.

2.8 The compliance score — three views and a penalty

A second, deeper score used after drafting. It builds three sub-scores:

completeness  = (rules covered ÷ total rules) × 100
technical     = 100 − (blocked × 12) − (weak × 4)
documentation = 100 − (blocked × 15) − (weak × 5)

Then blends them, with weights adding up to 1.0:

overall = completeness×0.55 + technical×0.25 + documentation×0.20 − (blocked × 2)

Notice a blocked rule is punished three times: once inside technical, once inside documentation, and once again at the end. That looks like a mistake. It is not. A blocked mandatory rule is the single strongest sign that a bid will be rejected, so the score is built to be dominated by it. Finally everything is clamped between 0 and 100, which is what stops that aggressive punishment producing a negative percentage.

2.9 The weakest algorithm in the program (and why we say so)

After drafting, we check whether each rule is actually covered. The current method: take everything written, and ask does the rule's exact wording appear inside it?

  • Appears twice (both title and description) → covered
  • Appears once → weak
  • Not at all → missing, or blocked if it was mandatory

This is not good enough, and it should be said plainly. It only finds exact copies. If the draft says "we will provide our tax clearance certificate" but the rule said "furnish evidence of tax compliance", it scores zero — even though the draft covers it perfectly.

It appears to work because drafts are generated from the rules, so the exact words often survive. But it is measuring copying, not covering.

The proper fix is to compare the set of words in each, rather than exact text — how much do they overlap? Two sentences sharing 80% of their meaningful words are almost certainly about the same thing, whatever order they are in.

This is in the guide because noticing the limits of your own work is the actual skill. Anyone can write something that works on the happy path.

2.10 Checking the writing quality

Three counters look through the draft for repeated phrases, generic waffle, and weak statements ("we will try to", "we hope to"). Each has its own penalty:

quality = 100 − (repeats × 12) − (generic × 10) − (weak × 8)

Honest about its limits: it can only find bad writing it has been told about. It will never spot a new kind of bad writing. In exchange, it runs in a thousandth of a second and never invents a problem that is not there.

That last one deserves its own mention, because you will need it any time you work with a system whose output you do not control:

Try to read the whole thing as data.        → worked? done.
Look for a ```json block and read that.     → worked? done.
Grab from the first { to the last }.        → worked? done.
Give up and say so clearly.

Getting a slightly wrong answer from a machine is far more common than getting a completely wrong one. Plan for slightly wrong.

Part 3

Backend engineering

The backend is everything that happens on the server — a computer somewhere else that you never see. The frontend is what runs on your own phone or laptop. When you press a button, the frontend asks the backend a question, and the backend answers.

The backend's job is to be the grown-up: it holds the real data, decides who is allowed to do what, and never believes anything the browser tells it.

3.1 Why never trust the browser

This is the first idea in security, and everything else depends on it.

Anything running on your computer, you control. You can open the developer tools and change it. So if the browser sends the server a message saying "I am the boss of this workspace, delete everything" — the server must not simply believe it.

So every private page and every action asks the server: who is this really, and are they allowed? The button being hidden in the interface is a convenience, not a protection. The protection is on the server.

3.2 The framework: Next.js

A framework is a pre-built skeleton for an app: routing, page building, and structure, so you do not write it all yourself.

We use Next.js, which lets one project contain both the pages you look at and the backend that serves them.

Why Next.js instead of the alternatives?

OptionWhat it meansWhy not
Next.js ✅Pages and backend in one projectChosen: one thing to build, deploy, and reason about
React on its ownJust the screensYou would still need a whole separate backend
Express + React separatelyTwo projectsTwo deployments, two sets of types, constant drift between them
Rails / DjangoMature all-in-one frameworksExcellent — but the team writes TypeScript, and mixing languages doubles what everyone must know

The deciding factor is real: one language everywhere. The same description of what a "Project" is gets used by the database, the backend, and the screen. If someone changes it in one place, the others stop compiling immediately — instead of breaking quietly in front of a customer.

3.3 The database: PostgreSQL and Prisma

A database stores information permanently and safely, even if the program restarts. PostgreSQL is one of the best free ones.

Our database has 25 tables. A table is like one spreadsheet: User, Project, Requirement, TenderClause, Payment, and so on.

Prisma sits between our code and the database. You describe your tables once in a single file, and Prisma:

  1. Creates the real tables for you.
  2. Generates code so that if you type project.titel instead of project.title,

it refuses to build — instead of returning nothing at 2am in production.

Why Prisma instead of the alternatives?

OptionWhy not
Prisma ✅Chosen: the safety net is automatic and complete
Writing SQL by handTotal control, zero safety net. One typo in a column name is a runtime bug
TypeORM / SequelizeOlder tools; their type safety has real gaps
DrizzleGenuinely good, lighter and closer to SQL. A fair alternative — Prisma won on maturity of its migration tooling

3.4 Migrations, and a real production accident

A migration is a recorded change to the database's shape — "add a column called country". They are numbered and run in order, so every copy of the database ends up identical.

There are 8 of them in this project.

What went wrong. One migration, add_organizations, ran halfway. It created some things and then failed. The database was left in a state that matched neither the "before" nor the "after".

The standard fix is a command that means "mark this as done and move on". It would have been the wrong thing to do, because the migration genuinely had not finished — every one of its eight connections between tables was missing. Marking it done would have buried the damage under a clean-looking record.

The lesson: when a tool offers you a quick fix for a broken state, first check which broken state you are actually in. The fix that clears the error message is not always the fix that solves the problem.

3.5 Sign-in, and how passwords are really stored

We never store your password. Not encrypted, not hidden — not at all.

Instead we store a hash: the result of a one-way scrambling recipe.

Feeding "hello" into a hash always gives the same scrambled result. But there is no way to run it backwards and get "hello" back. It is like blending fruit: easy one way, impossible to reverse.

When you sign in, we scramble what you typed and compare the two scrambles. If they match, you knew the password — without us ever having stored it. If our database is ever stolen, the thief gets scrambles, not passwords.

We use bcrypt with a cost of 12. The cost controls how slow the scrambling is: each step up doubles the time. Slowness is the feature — it makes each of an attacker's billions of guesses cost real time. Ours takes about a quarter of a second.

OptionWhy not
bcrypt ✅Chosen: everywhere, proven since 1999, deliberately slow
Plain textCatastrophic. Never
MD5 / SHA-1Built to be fast, which is exactly wrong here — a modern graphics card guesses billions per second
Argon2 / scryptGenuinely stronger, and resist specialised hardware better. bcrypt won on how boringly available it is everywhere

3.6 The best story in the project: the timing attack

This is a real security hole that existed in this code, and how it was found.

The setup. When you try to sign in, the server looks up your email. If there is no such account, there is nothing to compare against — so the obvious code just says "no" immediately.

The hole. Watch what an attacker sees:

Try alice@example.com  → "no"  after 266 milliseconds
Try zzz@nowhere.com    → "no"  after 0 milliseconds

Both say no. But the first one took a quarter of a second and the second was instant. Why? Because for Alice, the server did the slow scrambling work. For the made-up address, there was nothing to scramble, so it returned instantly.

The attacker has learned that Alice has an account here — without ever guessing her password. Do that with a million email addresses and you have a list of every customer. That is a genuine privacy breach, and it is often the first step in a targeted attack.

The fix is to always do the same amount of work. When there is no account, compare against a fake stored scramble anyway, so the slow part happens either way.

The twist, and why this story is worth telling. That fix was already in the code. There was a fake scramble, and a comment explaining it. But it had two problems:

  1. The fake was not a valid scramble — just a made-up string of letters. The

scrambling tool looks at it, sees it is malformed, and rejects it immediately without doing any work.

  1. It was set to cost 10, while real passwords used cost 12 — four times

faster even if it had been valid.

So the defence did nothing. Worse than nothing: it looked like protection, so nobody checked it. Measured:

SituationTime to say no
Real account, wrong password266 ms
No such account0 ms

The real lesson is not about passwords. It is this: a comment saying "this is safe" is not evidence. The hole was found by measuring, not by reading. And the tests written afterwards do not check that the fake scramble equals some particular text — they check the properties that matter: that it is a valid scramble, and that its cost matches the real one. If someone raises the cost later and forgets the fake, the tests fail instead of the hole silently reopening.

3.7 Stopping guessing: rate limits

If someone can try a million passwords, a slow hash only helps so much. So after 8 wrong attempts on one account, we stop accepting them for a few minutes.

An interesting decision. The obvious extra layer is "also block by internet address". We deliberately do not.

Most of this app's users are in Nigeria, on mobile networks. Those networks put thousands of unrelated people behind one shared address. Blocking by address would kick a whole city off together because one stranger typed a wrong password. Blocking per account gets the same protection without that.

That is a security decision made by knowing who the users are. The textbook answer would have been actively harmful here.

3.8 Checking passwords against real leaks — without sending them

The problem. P@ssw0rd1 passes every rule we have: eight characters, capital, lowercase, number, symbol. It has also appeared in 253,616 known data breaches. Rules alone cannot catch it.

There is a public service holding hundreds of millions of leaked passwords. But sending them your users' passwords would be insane.

The trick, called k-anonymity. Instead of asking about your password, ask about a whole neighbourhood of passwords.

1. Scramble your password           → 5BAA61E4C9B93F3F0682250B6CF8331B7EE68FD8
2. Send only the first 5 characters → "5BAA6"
3. They send back every leaked scramble starting with 5BAA6 — about 500 of them
4. We look through that list ourselves, on our own server

They never learn which one you asked about — or whether any of them matched. Your password never leaves the building.

And if that service is down? We let the password through. That is a deliberate choice: a person who cannot sign up because someone else's service is broken cannot fix it, cannot understand it, and simply leaves. A slightly weak password getting through is the smaller harm.

Fail open or fail closed? Failing closed (blocking when unsure) is right for a bank vault. Failing open is right for an optional extra check. Knowing which you are building is the skill.

3.9 Teams and permissions

Every person belongs to one or more organisations (workspaces). Every project belongs to an organisation, not to a person — so when someone leaves, the work does not leave with them.

Three roles:

RoleCan do
OwnerEverything, including billing and deleting the workspace
AdminInvite and remove people, delete projects and documents
MemberCreate and edit projects

Two careful details:

Admins cannot touch billing. Deliberately narrower than managing people, so you can let someone grow the team without giving them the payment method.

Admins cannot make someone an owner, or change an existing owner. Handing over ownership needs a proper flow that does not exist yet — so rather than half-support it, it is blocked.

3.10 Which workspace am I in? — a lesson about trust

The app remembers your current workspace in a cookie (a small note the browser keeps and sends back each time).

But a cookie lives on your computer, so you can change it. Someone could edit theirs to another workspace's identifier.

So the cookie is treated as a hint, never a fact. Every request the server re-checks: is this person actually a member of that workspace? If not, it quietly falls back to one they really do belong to. Not an error — a stale cookie should never lock you out, and it must never let you in.

3.11 A scheduled job that is not scheduled

Pro plans last 30 days. Something must notice when one runs out.

The obvious answer is a program that wakes every night and checks everyone. That is another moving part to run, monitor, and pay for.

Instead: the check happens the next time that workspace's data is loaded anyway. If the expiry date has passed, it downgrades right then. Nobody can experience an expired-but-still-working plan, because experiencing it requires loading the data, which triggers the check.

It only writes when a plan has genuinely expired, so it costs nothing the rest of the time. This is called lazy evaluation — do the work at the last useful moment, not on a schedule.

3.12 Payments, and two messages racing

After you pay, we find out in two ways:

  1. Your browser is redirected back to us.
  2. The payment company sends our server a direct message (a webhook).

Either can arrive first. Either can fail — you might close the tab straight after paying.

Both go to the same function, which first checks: is this payment already marked successful? If yes, it stops. So whichever arrives first does the work, and the other one harmlessly does nothing. Same idea as §2.6: doing it twice is the same as doing it once.

Two more safeguards:

The signature. The direct message is signed with a secret only we and the payment company know. Without checking it, anyone who found the address could send "this person paid!" and get a free upgrade.

The amount. We check the amount actually paid matches what we asked for. Never trust a number that travelled through the customer's browser.

3.13 The bug that broke every email for weeks

Every email — welcome, confirm your address, reset password, team invites, renewal reminders — failed. For weeks. Nobody noticed, because nothing errored loudly; the emails just never arrived.

The cause was one letter:

https://api.brevo.com/v3/smtp/emails      ← what we had.  Wrong.
https://api.brevo.com/v3/smtp/email       ← correct.

The wrong address returned 404 HTTP route not found — a gateway error, not an API error, so it carried no hint that the URL was the problem.

Why this is in the guide: it was misdiagnosed twice, blamed on a network restriction, before anyone simply sent the same message to both addresses and compared. Two minutes of measuring beat two rounds of confident theorising.

3.14 Doing slow work without making you wait

Reading an 80-page PDF and analysing it takes a while. If the upload request did all that before replying, you would stare at a spinner — and the server might give up and kill the request.

So the upload does the fast part (check the file, save it, record it), replies immediately, and the slow analysis runs after the reply has been sent, using a built-in feature for exactly this.

No queue system, no extra service. For this size of app, that is the right trade: fewer moving parts. If it grows, a real queue is the next step.

3.15 How we know it works: testing

A test is code that checks other code. You describe what should happen; the computer verifies it.

Two kinds here:

Unit tests (183) check one small piece with no database and no internet. They run in seconds, so you run them constantly.

Integration tests (67) start a real server and a real database, then do what a person would do: sign up, sign in, upload, export. Slower — about six minutes — but they catch things unit tests never can.

A story about why you need both. After a change, all 183 unit tests passed. Everything looked fine. Then the integration tests ran: 50 of 65 failed.

The cause was one thing — a helper that created test accounts used the password password123, which the new rules rejected. One root cause, fifty different-looking failures.

Worse, the helper that signed in did not check that it had worked. It returned whatever came back, so a refused sign-in showed up much later as a confusing error from something unrelated. It was fixed to fail loudly and immediately.

The lesson: a test helper that quietly tolerates failure turns one obvious bug into fifty mysterious ones.

Part 4

Frontend engineering

The frontend is everything you can see and touch. Its job is to make a complicated thing feel simple.

4.1 The single most important frontend idea here

There are two places work can happen: on the server, before the page is sent to you, or in your browser, after it arrives.

Think of a restaurant. Some things happen in the kitchen before the plate reaches you. Other things happen at your table — pouring water, adding salt. Both are needed. Doing kitchen work at the table is slow and messy.

In this app, 48 of 94 screen files run in your browser. The other half run only on the server. That is a deliberate default: server unless there is a reason.

Why prefer the server?

Server does the workBrowser does the work
Page arrives finishedPage arrives empty, then fills in
No delay while code downloadsYou wait for code, then wait for data
Database access is safe hereSecrets would be visible to anyone
Works on a slow phoneStruggles on a slow phone

The last row decided it. Many of this app's users are on mid-range Android phones on mobile data. Every kilobyte of code is a real wait for them.

Anything interactive — a form, a dropdown, the text editor — must run in the browser, and those files say "use client" at the top.

4.2 Forms: react-hook-form and Zod

A form seems easy until you list what it must do: remember what you typed, validate it, show errors next to the right box, disable the button while saving, and not re-draw the entire screen on every keystroke.

react-hook-form handles that. Its trick is that it does not re-draw the page as you type. Typing 40 characters redraws the page 40 times in the naive approach; this does it approximately never.

Zod describes what valid means, once:

signUpSchema = {
  name:        text, at least 2 characters
  email:       must look like an email
  password:    must pass the password rules
  country:     text, at least 2 characters
}

The valuable part: that same description is used in the browser and on the server. One definition, two places. They cannot disagree — and the server one is the one that actually protects you, because the browser can be bypassed.

OptionWhy not
react-hook-form + Zod ✅Chosen: fast, and one shared definition of valid
Plain React stateYou write all of it yourself, and re-draw constantly
FormikPopular, older, re-draws more
YupFine validator, weaker TypeScript support than Zod

4.3 A small bug worth learning from

The password checklist needed to watch what you were typing. The obvious way:

form.watch("password")

The code checker flagged it. React's newer optimiser cannot safely speed up code using watch(), because it cannot tell when the value really changed.

The fix was one word:

useWatch({ control: form.control, name: "password" })

Same result, and it subscribes to just that one field. The lesson: when a tool warns you about something that "works fine", it is usually warning about something that will stop working fine later.

4.4 Two kinds of memory

An app remembers two very different things.

Server state — things that live in the database: your projects, your requirements. The browser only ever holds a copy, which can go stale.

Client state — things that only matter right now, on your screen: which section is selected, whether a menu is open. Nobody else needs to know.

Beginners store both the same way, and it causes endless bugs. This app uses different tools for each:

  • TanStack Query for server state. It fetches, caches, and knows when a copy

is stale (after 30 seconds, here).

  • Zustand for client state. About twenty lines to hold "which section is

selected".

An honest note: TanStack Query is used in exactly one file. Most pages load their data on the server instead, which needs no fetching library at all. It earns its place for the editor and nowhere else. That is worth admitting — a dependency used once is a dependency worth questioning.

OptionWhy not
Zustand ✅Chosen: tiny, no ceremony
ReduxPowerful, lots of boilerplate; far too much for one selected id
React ContextFree, but re-draws everything underneath it on every change

4.5 The text editor: TipTap

The proposal editor needs bold, italic, headings, lists — and to give us the content back as structured data, not a screenshot.

TipTap provides that. It is a layer over a serious editing engine (ProseMirror), which is genuinely hard to use directly.

OptionWhy not
TipTap ✅Chosen: solid foundation, sane API
A plain <textarea>No formatting at all
contentEditable by handFamously the worst API in the browser
Quill / Draft.jsOlder, less actively maintained

4.6 Styling: Tailwind and design tokens

Tailwind lets you style by putting small named classes directly on elements: className="flex items-center gap-3" means "lay these out in a row, line them up, put a gap between them".

It looks cluttered at first. The payoff: you never invent a name for something you will only use once, and you cannot accidentally break a different page by editing a shared stylesheet.

Tokens are the important half. Colours are never written directly. Instead:

--primary: 176 76% 26%      ← in normal mode: a deep teal
--primary: 174 72% 42%      ← in dark mode:   a brighter teal

Everything says "use primary". Switching to dark mode changes those definitions once, and the whole app follows. Nothing anywhere says "teal".

4.7 Buttons and dropdowns: Radix

Building a dropdown that fully works is much harder than it looks. It must handle keyboard arrows, Escape, focus moving correctly, screen readers, and closing when you click away.

Radix provides these unstyled — all behaviour, no appearance. We add the appearance with Tailwind.

OptionWhy not
Radix ✅Chosen: correct behaviour, our own look
Build them ourselvesMonths of work, and accessibility bugs forever
Material UI / ChakraComplete, but bring their own look you then fight

4.8 One way to tell you something went wrong

Every message the app shows comes from one file, written under five rules:

  1. Never leak internal detail. CredentialsSignin is not a sentence.
  2. Never reveal whether an account exists.
  3. Say what happened and what to do next.
  4. Do not blame the reader. No "you entered it wrong". No exclamation marks.
  5. Do not over-apologise either. One clear sentence.

And one component draws them all, so a failure and a success never look like the same sentence in different colours. Each carries an icon as well as a colour, because colour alone is not readable by everyone — roughly 1 in 12 men has some colour blindness.

4.9 The bug that hid a fix

The best frontend bug in the project.

The plan: if sign-in fails because your email is unconfirmed, show a button offering a fresh link. The code did this:

if (the error message == "Confirm your email address before signing in...")
    show the button

It compared the shown error against the official message. Sensible.

But the same sentence existed in two files. One file threw the error; the other held the text the check compared against. They had drifted apart by a few words.

So the comparison never matched. The button would never have appeared. The escape hatch for locked-out users existed — and was unreachable.

It was caught by actually signing up and getting locked out, and noticing the message on screen did not match the one in the code.

Two lessons. First: never write the same sentence in two places; write it once and point at it. Second: a feature you have not used yourself is a feature you have not tested.

4.10 No flash of the wrong colour

If you use dark mode, a badly-built app flashes white for a moment before switching. That flash is the browser drawing the page before the code deciding your theme has run.

The fix: a tiny script that runs during the page's construction, before anything is drawn, and sets the theme immediately.

That script is a small security problem too — the app's security policy blocks inline scripts, exactly to stop injected ones. So this one is allowed by its fingerprint: a hash of its exact contents. Change one character and it stops being allowed. A test checks this, so a silent change breaks the build rather than the page.

Design

Design is not "making it pretty". It is making it understandable. A beautiful screen nobody can use has failed.

5.1 Colour, defined once

Every colour in the app has a name and a job:

NameJobNormal modeDark mode
backgroundThe page behind everythingNear-whiteNear-black
foregroundOrdinary textNear-blackNear-white
primaryThe brand; main buttonsDeep tealBrighter teal
muted-foregroundLess important textGreyLighter grey

Two things make this work:

Every colour comes in pairs. primary always travels with primary-foreground — the colour guaranteed to be readable on it. You never have to wonder whether white text will show up on that button.

The teal changes between modes. The deep teal that looks rich on white is too dark on a black page. So dark mode uses a brighter one. This is why "just invert the colours" produces bad dark modes — the relationships have to be rebuilt, not flipped.

5.2 The logo, and how it was picked

The app shipped for a long time with a teal square containing the letters "TF". A placeholder.

Ten designs were drawn and looked at. That mattered — several ideas that sounded good on paper failed the moment they were on screen:

IdeaWhat it actually looked like
An abstract "flowing T"A cursive lowercase t
Stacked chevronsThe fast-forward button on a video player
Stacked barsThe "align text" icon in a word processor
A shield with a tickAntivirus software
A folded page with rising bars ✅A document that is doing well

The winner says what the product is on sight — a document tool — which matters for something sitting in a list beside other business software.

The tie-break was a technical test. The runner-up was the same page with a rising arrow. The bars won because at 16 pixels — the size of the little icon in a browser tab — three separate vertical strokes stay readable, while a diagonal arrow blurs into a smudge.

The lesson: a logo is not judged at poster size. It is judged at the smallest size it must survive.

The mark is also drawn in code, not saved as a picture, so it can take its colours from the theme. A fixed-colour picture would either wash out on dark or glare on light.

5.3 Designing for email, which is a hostile place

Email is roughly 20 years behind the web. Modern layout tools do not work. Outlook uses Microsoft Word to draw emails.

The problem we hit. The logo was added as a single image at the top. It looked lovely — and then it was tested with images blocked, which is the default in most email programs. The header was completely empty: an image with a fixed 32-pixel size collapses to an empty box, and its alt text is clipped out of sight.

The fix: the mark stays an image, but the words "TenderFlow AI" are real text beside it.

Images onImages off
Logo + wordmarkWordmark still reads

And because the words are there, the image is marked decorative with empty alt text — otherwise a screen reader would announce the brand twice.

Other email-specific compromises, each with a reason:

  • Laid out with an old-fashioned <table>, because Outlook ignores modern

layout.

  • The image is sent at 128 pixels but shown at 32, so it stays sharp on

high-resolution screens.

  • Size is set as an attribute as well as a style, because Outlook ignores

the style.

  • Buttons are inline-block, without which some programs drop the padding and

the button collapses into plain underlined text.

5.4 Accessibility, as a design constraint

Not an add-on. Baked in:

  • Colour is never the only signal. Every status carries an icon or a shape

too.

  • Everything works from the keyboard. Some people cannot use a mouse.
  • Focus is always visible — you can see where you are.
  • Screen readers get the right announcements: errors interrupt; confirmations

wait their turn.

  • Motion can be turned off, respecting the setting people with vestibular

disorders use.

One small, thoughtful detail: the show-password eye is deliberately skipped when you press Tab. Someone tabbing out of the password field expects the sign-in button, not a control they never asked for. It is still reachable by clicking, and still announced properly.

5.5 Words are design too

Compare:

The right-hand column tells you what happened and what to do. It also never blames you and never shouts. There is a test that fails the build if any message contains an exclamation mark or the phrase "you failed" — because tone is the thing that quietly rots as a codebase grows.

Part 6

Product management

Product management answers: what should we build, for whom, and why not the other thing? Every "yes" is a "no" to something else.

6.1 The problem, stated precisely

A small company in Nigeria wants to bid for a contract. The rulebook is 80 pages. Missing one rule means instant rejection. Big competitors have whole teams for this; the small company has one person doing it at midnight.

Notice what that sentence contains: who (small companies), where (Nigeria), what hurts (rejection over a missed rule), and why they cannot just try harder (no team). A problem stated that precisely answers most design questions on its own.

6.2 The constraints that shaped everything

Real projects are shaped more by limits than by ambitions:

ConstraintWhat it forced
No money to spend until customers payEvery service must have a free tier and a working fallback
Nigerian marketPaystack, not Stripe. Prices in Naira
Users on mobile dataServer-rendered pages; small downloads
Solo developerOne codebase, one language, no microservices
Enterprise buyersAudit logs, teams, roles, privacy and terms pages — before they will sign

That first row explains a surprising amount. AI has a local fallback. Storage has a local fallback. Rate limiting has a local fallback. Not architectural purity — the inability to pay for reliability, turned into a design principle that turned out to be better anyway.

6.3 The order things were built in

Ordering is most of product management. This is roughly what happened:

  1. Upload a document and get requirements out of it. The core magic. Without

this the product has no reason to exist.

  1. Show the checklist. The magic made visible.
  2. Accounts and projects. Now people can keep their work.
  3. Drafting. Move from "tells me what to do" to "helps me do it".
  4. Export. Without a file to submit, the whole thing is a toy.
  5. Payments. Only once it was worth paying for.
  6. Teams. Only once single users were happy.
  7. Security hardening. Before real customer data arrives.

Why this order? Each step is useless without the one before it. Building teams before drafting would mean several people collaborating on nothing.

A common mistake is building step 6 first, because payments feel like "the business". You cannot charge for a product that does not work yet.

6.4 Free limits as a product decision, not a technical one

Starter (free)Pro
Document uploads / month100500
AI generations / month10100
PriceFree₦5,000 / month

The interesting number is 10 AI generations. That is enough to genuinely finish one real bid — so a new user can experience winning something, not just sampling. It is not enough to run a bid business on. The free tier is designed to prove the value, then run out at exactly the point where the product has started earning its keep.

The limit is a soft cap: it blocks a new action once you have hit the limit, rather than refusing an action that might exceed it. Since one request can produce several sections, predicting is unreliable — so it does not try.

6.5 Decisions that cost usability to earn trust

Three places where the harder path was chosen, with the cost stated honestly.

1. Signup will not tell you if an email is already registered.

The cost: someone who forgot they had an account gets a confusing "check your inbox" instead of "you already have an account".

The reason: otherwise anyone can type emails into your signup form and learn who your customers are. For a product where customers are companies bidding on contracts, "who uses TenderFlow" is commercially sensitive.

The compromise: the answer still gets delivered — by email, to the person who actually owns the address. They are told an account exists, with links to sign in or reset. And if it was not them, they learn someone else just tried.

2. New accounts must confirm their email before signing in.

The cost: one extra step, and total dependence on email delivery.

The reason: an unconfirmed account can be created for an address you do not own.

The compromise: it applies only to new accounts. Everyone who signed up earlier is unaffected — verified against the real database before shipping, where 0 existing accounts were locked out. And because email had genuinely been broken for weeks, there is now a way to request a fresh link without signing in — otherwise the people who most needed it would have been the ones who could not ask.

3. Sign-in does not tell you which part was wrong.

The cost: slightly less helpful.

The reason: "no account with that email" is a free membership check for anyone who asks.

Knowing what to leave out matters as much as what to include:

Real-time collaborative editingVery hard; bid teams are small and rarely type at once
A job queueCurrent volumes do not need one. Real complexity, real cost
Automatic recurring billingNeeds a payment relationship that does not exist yet; renewal reminders cover it
Mobile appsThe website works on phones. Two more codebases would not pay for themselves
OCR for scanned PDFsGenuinely wanted, genuinely expensive. For now the app says clearly it cannot read the file, which is better than pretending

That last row is the pattern worth copying: when you cannot solve something, failing clearly beats failing silently.

6.7 How quality is actually kept

Not by intention — by making the computer check:

  • 250 automated tests across the two tiers.
  • Type checking — the computer proves the pieces fit before it will build.
  • Linting — an automatic reviewer catching known mistakes.
  • **Tests that pin behaviour, not text.** The password placeholder test checks

it is valid and correctly costly, not that it equals a particular string. So the test still protects you after someone edits the value.

  • Tone tests. The build fails if a user-facing message contains an

exclamation mark or blames the reader.

That last one is unusual and worth stealing. Writing quality decays as a codebase grows and many hands touch it. Making it a test makes it survive.

Appendix A

Every package, explained

A package (or library) is code somebody else wrote that you borrow instead of writing it yourself. You list the ones you want, and a tool downloads them. Every package is a trade. You get working code for free — and you take on someone else's bugs, someone else's security holes, and a download your users must wait for. So each one should earn its place.

This project has 41 packages it ships with and ~19 development tools. Here is every one.

The foundation

**next** — The framework. Provides page routing, server rendering, the API routes, and the build system. Everything else sits on it. Why not alternatives: Remix is excellent and similar; Astro is better for content sites but weaker for app-like interactivity; plain React would require building the backend separately. Next won on having pages and backend in one project.

**react and react-dom** — The library that turns data into screens. You describe what the screen should look like for some data, and React works out the minimum changes needed. react is the thinking; react-dom is the part that actually touches the browser. Why not alternatives: Vue and Svelte are both very good — Svelte produces smaller downloads. React won on the size of its ecosystem: TipTap, Radix, react-hook-form and TanStack Query all exist for React first.

**typescript** — Adds types to JavaScript. You say "this is a number", and if you later treat it as text, the computer refuses to build. Why it matters here: the same shapes travel from database to server to screen. Types are what make a change in one place break loudly in the others, instead of quietly in front of a customer.

Talking to the database

**prisma (tool) and @prisma/client** (code) — You describe your tables once; Prisma creates them and generates type-safe code to read and write them. Why not: raw SQL gives total control and zero safety; TypeORM and Sequelize have weaker type safety; Drizzle is a genuinely good lighter alternative that lost on migration tooling maturity.

**@prisma/adapter-pg** — The piece that lets Prisma talk to PostgreSQL specifically.

**pg** — The low-level PostgreSQL driver the adapter uses underneath. You never call it directly.

Signing in and staying safe

**next-auth** — Handles sessions: proving who you are and remembering it across pages. Manages the cookies, the tokens, and the sign-in flow. Why not: Clerk and Auth0 are excellent and cost money — impossible under a no-spend constraint. Rolling your own session handling is a classic way to ship a security hole. Honest note: this is v4. v5 exists but was in beta when this was built, and authentication is the wrong place to be an early adopter.

**@auth/prisma-adapter** — Connects next-auth to our database, so sessions and accounts are stored in our own tables.

**bcryptjs** — Scrambles passwords one-way (§3.5). The js means it is written in pure JavaScript, so it installs anywhere without needing compilation — which matters on serverless hosts. Why not: the native bcrypt is faster but needs compiling per platform. Argon2 is stronger but the same install problem, worse.

**@upstash/ratelimit and @upstash/redis** — Counting how many times someone has tried something, in a shared place all servers can see. Redis is a very fast store; Upstash is a version that works over plain web requests, which serverless functions need. Why not: a normal Redis needs a permanent connection, which serverless functions cannot hold. Counting in memory does not work when each request may land on a different machine.

Reading and writing documents

**pdf-parse** — Pulls text out of PDFs. Its limitation, which shapes the product: it can only read PDFs that contain text. A scanned page is a photograph, and no text-extractor can read it. That is why the app detects this and says so, rather than showing an empty checklist.

**mammoth** — Pulls text out of Word .docx files. Chosen because it converts to clean, simple output rather than trying to reproduce Word's formatting mess.

**docx** — Creates Word files, for export. Builds real .docx documents that open properly in Word, Google Docs, and LibreOffice.

**pdfkit** — Creates PDF files, for export. Why two export libraries: because buyers demand specific formats, and "just send a PDF" is not always allowed.

The AI part

**@google/genai** — The official client for Google's Gemini models. Used to read tender documents more intelligently than our own recipes can, and to help draft proposals. Why Gemini: it has a genuinely usable free tier, which was decisive under the no-spend constraint. OpenAI and Anthropic models are strong alternatives; both cost money from the first request. How it is used carefully: the app asks for JSON specifically, validates the shape of what comes back with Zod, and if anything at all goes wrong, silently falls back to the local recipes.

Storing files

**@supabase/supabase-js** — Talks to Supabase, which provides both the PostgreSQL database and file storage on a free tier. Why it is needed: most modern hosting has a read-only filesystem. You cannot simply save an uploaded file next to your code. It has to go somewhere built for it. The fallback: if Supabase is not configured, files save to local disk — which is fine for development and would lose files in production. The code says so plainly in a comment.

Building screens

**react-hook-form** — Form state without redrawing on every keystroke (§4.2).

**zod** — Describes what valid data looks like, and checks it. Used for form input, API input, and validating AI responses. The special thing about it: the description is also the type. One definition gives you both the runtime check and the compile-time type — they cannot drift. Why not: Yup is similar with weaker TypeScript; Joi is server-only; hand-written checks drift from your types immediately.

**@hookform/resolvers** — The small adapter letting react-hook-form use a Zod description directly.

**@radix-ui/*** (7 packages) — Unstyled, fully accessible building blocks: alert-dialog (confirm before deleting), checkbox, popover (the account menu), select (dropdowns), tabs, tooltip, and slot (a utility for composing components). Why unstyled matters: you get years of accessibility work — keyboard handling, focus management, screen reader support — and still control every pixel.

**@tanstack/react-query** — Fetching and caching server data (§4.4). Used in one file.

**zustand** — Small client-side state (§4.4). About 1KB.

**@tiptap/react and @tiptap/starter-kit** — The rich text editor (§4.5). starter-kit bundles the usual extensions: bold, italic, headings, lists.

**lucide-react** — The icons. Clean, consistent, and imported one at a time so only the ones used get downloaded. Why not: Font Awesome ships enormous files; Heroicons is smaller but has fewer icons; emoji are inconsistent across platforms and read badly to screen readers.

Styling

**tailwindcss** — Styling with small utility classes (§4.6).

**tailwindcss-animate** — Adds animation utilities, used for menus and dialogs appearing.

**clsx** — Joins class names together conditionally. clsx("base", isActive && "active").

**tailwind-merge** — Solves a real Tailwind problem: if two conflicting classes end up on one element (p-2 and p-4), which wins is unpredictable. This resolves them properly, keeping the last. Together these two make the cn() helper used throughout the codebase.

**class-variance-authority** — Lets a component have named variants — a button that is primary or destructive, small or large — without a tangle of conditionals.

**postcss and autoprefixer** — Build-time CSS tools. PostCSS is the processor Tailwind runs inside; autoprefixer adds the browser-specific prefixes some CSS still needs.

Keeping it running

**@sentry/nextjs** — Error tracking. When something breaks for a real user, this records what happened and where. Why it matters: without it you find out about bugs when someone complains, which is both late and rare — most people just leave.

**dotenv** — Loads settings (database address, API keys) from a .env file, so secrets live outside the code and never reach version control.

Development tools

**eslint, eslint-config-next, @eslint/eslintrc** — The automatic code reviewer. Catches unused variables, likely mistakes, and framework-specific errors. This is what caught the watch() problem in §4.3.

**tsx** — Runs TypeScript files directly, without compiling first. Used for scripts and the test runner.

**@types/*** (7 packages) — Type descriptions for libraries written in plain JavaScript, so TypeScript can still check your use of them.

Two honest admissions

**better-sqlite3 and @prisma/adapter-better-sqlite3 — Leftovers from when the project used SQLite before moving to PostgreSQL. They appear in the build configuration but nothing imports them**.

**framer-motion (animation) and date-fns (date formatting) — Both declared, both used in zero files**. Verified by searching the whole codebase.

That is four packages being downloaded and installed for nothing. Not a catastrophe, but worth knowing: dependency lists rot. Things get added during an experiment and never removed. Checking occasionally is a real maintenance task, and admitting it here is more useful than a tidy list that hides it.

Known weaknesses

Coverage detection uses exact text matching (§2.9). It measures copying, not covering. The fix is word-overlap comparison.

Quality review only finds problems on its list. A blocklist cannot detect anything it has not been told about.

No OCR. Scanned PDFs cannot be read at all. The app says so clearly, which is the right behaviour, but it is a real limitation for government documents that are often scans.

Rate limiting needs Upstash configured in production. Without it the counting happens in memory, and on serverless hosting each request may land on a different machine — so the limit barely binds. The code warns loudly about this rather than failing silently.

TanStack Query is used once. Worth either using more or removing.

Operational things to know

**NEXT_PUBLIC_APP_URL must be the real deployed address.** Email links, invite links, and the logo in emails are all built from it. A wrong value means links that go nowhere.

Email delivery is a single point of failure. New accounts cannot sign in until they confirm by email. If email breaks, signup effectively breaks. This is why the "send me a new link" page exists without needing a sign-in.

**middleware is deprecated in Next 16.** It still works, and it is what enforces the security policy, but it will need renaming to proxy eventually.
